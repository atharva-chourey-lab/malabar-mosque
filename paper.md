# An attestation ladder for India's first mosque: the Cheraman tradition against the dated record

Author: Atharva Chourey
Affiliation: Atharva Chourey Lab (independent research lab)

*Manuscript in preparation — not yet peer-reviewed.*

## Abstract

The Cheraman Juma Masjid at Methala, Kodungallur, is by tradition the first mosque in India, founded in 629 CE after the last Cheraman Perumal witnessed the splitting of the moon, accepted Islam at the hands of the Prophet Muhammad, and authorised his companions to build mosques at the Malabar ports. This paper grades that tradition against the dated record: the legend's own earliest texts, the standing fabric, the Arabic geographical witnesses in date order, and the coast's dated epigraphy. The Prophet-lifetime synchronism that produces 629 is the youngest layer of the tradition, not the earliest: Duarte Barbosa (c. 1516) dates the king's conversion to c. 900, João de Barros to c. 886, and Zainuddin's *Tuḥfat al-Mujāhidīn* (1583), the first full devotional telling, places it two centuries after the Prophet, c. 820s; the named founder, Malik ibn Dinar, died c. 748, a generation after the Companions, and cannot have been commissioned by the Prophet. The dated record instead gives a probable Muslim burial on the coast in 782/783, a firm Muslim community witnessing a royal charter at Kollam in 849, and the earliest dated mosque foundation in Kerala in 1124, while the Kodungallur mosque's oldest datable fabric is its granite foundation, stylistically 11th–12th century. The verdicts are therefore: a foundation in 629 is rejected as dated; a mosque at Kodungallur by the 11th–12th century is probable, and by the 14th–15th century firm. Read coast-wide, every community's tradition overshoots its attestation — Christians 52 to 849, Jews 379 to c. 1000, Muslims 629 to 849 — and all three first become visible in the same document, the Quilon plates of 849.

*Keywords:* Cheraman Juma Masjid; Kodungallur; Malabar coast; Cheraman Perumal; Malik ibn Dinar; Quilon plates; mosque epigraphy; legend stratigraphy; attestation

## 1. The claim under test

The Cheraman Juma Masjid at Methala, Kodungallur (Cranganore), Kerala, is by tradition the first mosque in India, founded in 629 CE — 7 AH in the common version; the mosque's own displays variously say 5, 7 and 9 AH — by Malik ibn Dinar, after the last Cheraman Perumal, the Chera sovereign of Kodungallur, witnessed the splitting of the moon, sailed to Arabia, met and accepted Islam at the hands of the Prophet Muhammad, and died at Zafar on the return journey, leaving letters that authorised his companions to build mosques at the Malabar ports.

This paper applies a single method to that claim: foreign dated records and dated local epigraphy as anchors for a tradition that does not date itself; stratigraphy by dated witness; verdicts graded, with negatives published. A 629 event whose first text is sixteenth-century is a stratigraphy problem: date every motif's first appearance, and a motif's first appearance is its terminus, not its truth.

The evidence base and its limits are stated at the outset. The paper rests on the published scholarship and survey literature: Shokoohy (2003) on the architecture; Narayanan on the Perumals and the plates; the standard Mappila-history surveys for the tombstone and mosque inscriptions; and J. B. P. More on the legend's textual history. Epigraphic details reported only through secondary surveys — the Panthalayani tombstone, the Koyilandy inscription — are graded *probable*, not *firm*, and flagged where the secondary literature contradicts itself. One such contradiction is itself a finding (§4.4): the tombstone's date, Hijra 166, is repeatedly printed as "752 CE" in the secondary literature; AH 166 is 782–783 CE.

The headline result is that the legend's own earliest witnesses do not tell the 629 story. Barbosa (c. 1516) dates the king's conversion to roughly 600 years before his own time — c. 900 CE — with no Prophet, no moon and no Malik ibn Dinar. Zainuddin (1583) himself places the event two centuries after the Prophet. Meanwhile the coast's dated documents put a Muslim community on the ground by 849 CE — eleven Kufic signatures witnessing a royal charter at Kollam — and a probable Muslim burial by 782/783. The mosque at Kodungallur has no dated element earlier than an 11th–12th-century granite foundation.

## 2. The tradition, layered — motif by motif, first appearance first

### 2.1 Duarte Barbosa (composed c. 1516–1518) — the first written version

Barbosa, who lived in Malabar from 1500 and learned Malayalam, is the earliest writer to record the story at all — nearly nine centuries after the alleged event. His version has a mighty king, "Chirimay Perumal," ruling a pepper port, converted to Islam by Moorish traders through discussion, who sailed with them to Mecca, where he died, or on the way back; Kerala never saw him again. On his departure the single kingdom was partitioned among Cannanore, Calicut and Quilon. Barbosa dates the event to "more than 600 years before our time" — i.e. c. 900 CE, not 875 years back to 629. His informants, in c. 1510, placed their own foundation story in the 9th–10th century. Absent from Barbosa are the Prophet in person, the moon-splitting, Malik ibn Dinar, letters, a mosque list, and Kodungallur named as first mosque.

### 2.2 João de Barros (1552–1563)

Barros dates the king ("Sarama Perumal") to 612 years before the Portuguese landing of 1498 — c. 886 CE. Two independent Portuguese notices, working from Malabar informants, both land the event in the late 9th century. Neither knows anything of 629.

### 2.3 Zainuddin Makhdum II, *Tuḥfat al-Mujāhidīn* (completed c. 1583) — the first full devotional telling

The first history of Kerala written by a Keralite, in Arabic, by the Ponnani scholar Zainuddin (d. after 1583). This is where the received story is assembled. A party of poor Muslim pilgrims, led by a shaykh, stops at Kodungallur on its way to the Footprint of Adam in Ceylon; the king entertains them; the shaykh tells him of the Prophet and of the splitting of the moon; the king believes, departs secretly with them, and dies abroad. The companions return bearing the king's letters; the local rulers honour them; Malik ibn Dinar, Sharaf ibn Malik, Malik ibn Habib and their families arrive and found mosques at a named string of ports — Kodungallur first, then Kollam, Madayi, Barkur, Mangalore, Kasaragod, Kannur, Dharmadam, Panthalayani Kollam, Chaliyam, with lists varying slightly.

Crucially, Zainuddin explicitly does *not* put the event in the Prophet's lifetime. As J. B. P. More's reading of the *Tuḥfat* established, and as the Kerala historiography repeatedly notes, Zainuddin places the conversion two hundred years after the Prophet — c. 820s CE. The earliest full text of the legend disagrees with the date now carved on the mosque's board.

### 2.4 *Qiṣṣat Shakarwati Farmād* (anonymous Arabic manuscript, undated)

The romance of "Chakrawati Farmas" — the king who saw the moon split, travelled, converted at the Prophet's hand, and died at Zafar — is preserved in a British Library / India Office manuscript (Arabic 2607) and was publicised by Muhammad Hamidullah. It is routinely cited as the "earliest" version because it is the fullest early-looking one. It is undated, and nothing in its transmission shows it older than the 16th–17th-century materials around it. An undated manuscript cannot outrank dated ones; it is graded here as evidence for the legend's developed form, not for its antiquity.

### 2.5 *Keralolpathi* (compiled 17th–18th century; extant manuscripts later)

The Malayalam Brahminical charter tradition absorbs the same departure from the Hindu side: the last Perumal divides Kerala among his heirs and leaves for Mecca, in some recensions renamed Tajuddin, resident five years in Arabia, dying in Yemen. The *Keralolpathi* is doing charter work: the Perumal's exit is the charter for Kerala's entire post-Perumal political order — the 64 settlements, the twelve-year Perumals, the partition among successor chiefs. The Mecca destination is one variant of the exit; the older Hindu variant, already fixed in the 12th-century *Periyāpurāṇam*, has the Perumal saint (Cheraman Perumal Nayanar) ascend to Kailasa with Sundarar from Kodungallur itself. The "departing Perumal who does not return" is a structural slot in Kerala's charter mythology, filled by Kailasa in the Shaivite stratum and by Mecca in the Muslim stratum — centuries before either was written as "history."

### 2.6 The mosque's own displays (19th–21st century)

A stone marker gives the foundation as 9 AH / 629 CE — the pairing is itself loose: 9 AH is 630–631 CE; 7 AH is the year that overlaps 629. Popular accounts variously give 5, 7 or 9 AH. An Arabi-Malayalam inscription on the gate claims 5 AH. This is self-refuting as an artefact: Arabi-Malayalam — Malayalam written in a modified Arabic script — is a medieval-to-early-modern development of the Mappila community. A 7th-century foundation inscription could not be written in it. The plaque is a late carving of an early claim. The developed modern form of the story — the king meets the Prophet, presents ginger pickle, performs the Farewell Pilgrimage (632), is named Tajuddin — crystallises in local tradition in the 18th–20th centuries and is the version on the mosque's board today.

And one impossibility inside the cast list: the historical Malik ibn Dinar (d. c. 748 CE) was a Basran *tābiʿī* — a man of the generation after the Companions, a disciple of Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, born decades after 629 on any chronology of his life. He cannot have been commissioned by the Prophet (d. 632) or have built a mosque in 629. The Kerala founder is either a different, otherwise unknown man carrying the name, or the name itself was borrowed for its prestige — the standard scholarly reading (Shokoohy) is the latter. The founder's name is an anachronism of the same kind as the date.

## 3. The fabric — what the standing building can be made to say

The mosque stands at Methala, Kodungallur, in the historic core of the old port of Muziris/Mahodayapuram. What is actually datable in it is as follows.

*Plan and form.* A Kerala temple-type mosque: laterite-and-timber construction, sloping tiled roofs, a prayer hall in the local idiom, a pond, a wooden minbar with carved and lacquered work, a great brass hanging lamp (the "Cheraman lamp") of temple type. Nothing in the form is Arabian or 7th-century; everything in it is Malabar — which the tradition itself celebrates, but which also means the form cannot be dated by foreign parallels, only by local ones.

*Architectural dating.* Mehrdad Shokoohy, in the standard survey (*Muslim Architecture of South India*, 2003, pp. 139–142), dates the surviving traditional structure's type and fabric to the 14th–15th century. The survey literature on Malabar mosques dates the mosque's granite foundation to the 11th–12th century on architectural style. These two are the only professional datings of the building itself, and they agree on the order of magnitude: the oldest datable element is medieval, roughly half a millennium after 629.

*Phasing.* Local tradition itself records repeated rebuilding: a renovation assigned to the 11th century, a major rebuild conventionally dated 1568, renovations in 1974 and 2001, a concrete extension with minarets and dome added in 1984 — which for the first time made the building look like a North-Indian-style mosque — and a restoration completed in 2022 under the Muziris Heritage Project that returned the exterior to the traditional Kerala form. A timber-and-tile building in the monsoon zone is rebuilt on a cycle of centuries; "the mosque of 629" as a site and congregation and "the mosque of 629" as a building are different claims, and only the first is even arguable.

*Inscriptions in the mosque.* There is no foundation inscription with a date — no Hijri or Kollam-era date carved at any foundation moment. The dated texts the mosque displays are the modern plaques discussed in §2.6. A claim circulating in polemical literature that "an inscription in the mosque dates it to 1124" appears to conflate Kodungallur with the Madayi mosque's dated slab of AH 518 / 1124 CE (see §4.3). The brass lamp carries an inscription in old Malayalam (Vattezhuthu) script; no published reading dates it to the early period, and Vattezhuthu remained in use for a millennium, so the script alone cannot carry a 7th-century date.

*Artefacts.* The wooden minbar, the lamp, and a block of white marble said to have been brought from Mecca are all claimed antiquities; none is dated by inscription or by any published technical study. They are evidence of the tradition's material culture, not of its date.

Fabric verdict: the oldest datable element of the Cheraman Juma Masjid is its granite foundation, stylistically 11th–12th century; the oldest surviving superstructure type is 14th–15th century (Shokoohy). No element of the building, and no inscription in it, reaches within four centuries of 629.

## 4. The Arab witnesses — and the epigraphic floor beneath them

The Arabic geographical tradition describes this coast lovingly and in detail for six centuries. What it does and does not say is the sharpest external check on the legend.

The silence is the datum: across five centuries of Arabic writing about this exact coast — its ports, its kings, its pepper, its customs dues — no source mentions a converted Malabar king, a mosque at Kodungallur, or Malik ibn Dinar in India. The legend enters writing from the Malabar side, in the 16th century, at the moment when the Portuguese arrival made a charter of first-arrival worth writing down.

### 4.2 What the witnesses do establish

Arab trade presence on the coast is as old as the sources: Sulaiman's 851 itinerary is itself an artefact of a mature Siraf–Kollam–Canton system. Trade presence, however, is the weakest form of the claim — pre-Islamic Arab and Persian shipping had run to Muziris for centuries (the *Periplus* already knows this coast in the 1st century CE), and sailors in port are not a settled community. The witnesses support: shipping by 851 (firm); a settled mosque-building community on the west coast by c. 915 (Masudi, at Chaul); Malabar mosques as established institutions by the 1340s (Battuta).

### 4.3 The epigraphic floor (dated objects, not texts about the past)

- **782/783 CE (AH 166)** — tombstone outside the Juma mosque at Panthalayani Kollam (north Malabar) recording the death of a Muslim, ibn Udthorman. A Muslim buried on the coast implies a community that buries its own. Graded **probable**: the reading circulates through the survey literature rather than a modern epigraphic edition.
- **AH 50 / 670 CE** — a tombstone at Irikkalur near Valapattanam reported by C. N. Ahammad Moulavi, now lost. Unverifiable; recorded here because the rule is to publish the claim and its grade, not to delete it. Grade: reported-only.
- **Umayyad period (issues of 661–750)** — a few Umayyad coins found at Kothamangalam, inland of Kodungallur. Coins attest that Arab-world money reached the coast in the Umayyad century; coins travel in purses, not in congregations. Trade contact: yes. Settlement: not shown.
- **849 CE** — the Quilon (Tarisapalli) copper plates: eleven Muslim witnesses sign, in Kufic Arabic, a Chera royal charter granting privileges to the Christian church at Kollam — alongside witnesses signing in Pahlavi and Hebrew. This is the earliest firm attestation of a Muslim community on the Malabar coast: not sailors passing, but named local notables of sufficient standing to witness a royal deed, in 849.
- **10th century** — an Old Malayalam inscription (Vattezhuthu with Grantha) in the Koyilandy Jumu'ah Mosque, north Malabar: a mosque building carrying a 10th-century epigraphic presence. Graded probable (survey literature).
- **10th–11th century** — the Panthalayani Kollam mosque preserves two royal charters of the Kodungallur Chera king Bhaskara Ravi Manukuladitya (fl. 962–1021), one built into the steps of the mosque tank: a functioning mosque institution in possession of royal charters by the medieval centuries.
- **1124 CE (AH 518)** — the Arabic foundation inscription (copper slab) of the Madayi Mosque: the earliest dated mosque-foundation inscription in Kerala. Mosque foundations on this coast become epigraphically visible in the 12th century, not the 7th.
- **13th century** — the Muchundi Mosque at Kozhikode and its inscription; **14th century** — the Kuttichira Jama Masjid at Calicut, whose first qadi's tenure is remembered as 1343–1370, dovetailing with Battuta's eyewitness account.

### 4.4 A conversion error in the wild

The Panthalayani tombstone's date, AH 166, is printed as "752 CE" in a peer-reviewed journal article on Arab accounts of Malabar and repeated downstream. AH 166 runs from October 782 to September 783 CE; 752 CE falls in AH 134–135. The misprint silently moves the coast's first Muslim burial thirty years earlier and into the Umayyad period's most suggestive decade. It changes no verdict here — 782/783 and 752 are both two-plus centuries before the documents thicken — but it is a clean specimen of how traditional dates get "corroborated": an error that flatters the early date is copied; one that didn't, wouldn't be.

## 5. The king problem — a title in search of a man

"Cheraman Perumal" is not a name. *Cheraman* marks the Chera lineage; *Perumal* is "the Great One" — the regnal title of the sovereigns of the medieval Chera kingdom of Mahodayapuram (Kodungallur). A dynastic title carries zero synchronistic weight until it is individuated to a named king with independent dates, and every attempt to individuate this one lands centuries away from 629.

The structural anachronism is prior to any candidate. The Chera Perumal kingdom of Mahodayapuram itself runs c. 800–1124 CE (Narayanan; Veluthat). In 629 there was no Cheraman Perumal at Kodungallur in the sense the legend requires: the Sangam Chera order had faded by about the 5th century, and the 7th century is Kerala's epigraphic dark age — no inscription names any king at Kodungallur in or near 629. The legend presupposes a court, a capital and a title that the record first shows two centuries later.

| Candidate | Dates | Basis of the identification | Gap to 629 |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Bhaskara Ravi Varma, r. 622–628" (the mosque's popular tradition) | As claimed | Circular: the regnal years are manufactured by back-projection from the legend itself; no inscription knows this king in these years | 0 — by construction, which is the problem |
| Bhaskara Ravi Varma of the inscriptions | fl. 962–1021 | The real, epigraphically dense Bhaskara Ravi: issuer of the Jewish copper plates (38th regnal year → c. 1000) and of the charters preserved at Panthalayani mosque. The name the tradition reaches for belongs to him | ~350 yrs too late |
| Cheraman Perumal Nayanar, the Shaivite saint-king | 8th–9th c. (traditional) | The *Periyāpurāṇam* (12th c.) departure legend — to Kailasa, not Mecca. Shows the departing-Perumal motif attached to the title centuries before any Mecca version is written; the two legends compete for the same slot | ~200 yrs too late, and the wrong destination |
| Rama Varma Kulasekhara, the last Perumal | r. c. 1090–1122 | The historical end of the Perumal line c. 1122–1124 and the partition of authority among successor chiefs — the most plausible kernel for a "last Perumal departs and divides the kingdom" memory, with the destination swapped between Kailasa and Mecca recensions | ~500 yrs too late |

The honest middle position is Narayanan's, and it is routinely misquoted as support for 629. Narayanan wrote that there is "no reason to reject the tradition that a Cheraman Perumal embraced Islam and went to Mecca" — but his own chronological framework contains no Perumal before c. 800, so the tradition he declines to reject is a conversion event in the 9th–12th-century window: exactly where Barbosa's informants (c. 900) and Zainuddin's own dating (c. 820s) put it, and where the epigraphy (Muslim witnesses at a Chera court, 849) makes a court-connected conversion conceivable. Shokoohy, from the architecture, is blunter: the legend *as dated* is fictitious. No epigraphist has produced a 7th-century candidate, because the 7th century at Kodungallur produces no kings at all.

The conversion cycle is also portable. Tamil Muslim tradition carries parallel versions on the Coromandel side (documented by J. B. P. More), where the Palaiya Jumma Palli at Kilakarai likewise claims a foundation in the Prophet's lifetime, and the departing-king motif attaches to different kings in different ports. A motif that can be localised to whichever port is telling it behaves as a charter of precedence — each old port's claim to have been first — not as the record of one datable event. The portability does not disprove that *some* Malabar king converted; it dissolves the link between the motif and any single king, port or year.

Tradition says 629; the first text is 1516; the first foreign witness to Malabar Islam is 1342. The ladder is the argument in one view: each rung is a dated object or a dated text, and the 629 rung has neither.

## 7. The web — one coast, four ladders

Kodungallur–Kollam is the densest correlation node in early India: four communities claim their Indian beginning on this one short coast, and for once the coast answers with dated objects.

| Community | Tradition date | First local dated document | First foreign witness | What the documents actually establish |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Christians (Thomas) | 52 CE (arrival of the Apostle) | 849 CE — the Tarisapalli church at Kollam receives a Chera royal charter | c. 550 — Cosmas Indicopleustes reports Christians in "Male" (Malabar) with clergy linked to Persia | A Persian-linked church institution at Kollam by 849; Christians on the coast by c. 550. The apostolic foundation is unattested by any document within 500 years of its date |
| Jews | 379 CE (traditional date of the plates; arrivals pushed to 68/70 CE in later tradition) | c. 1000 CE — the Joseph Rabban plates themselves, dated by epigraphy and by Bhaskara Ravi's 38th regnal year; but Jewish witnesses already sign (in Hebrew) at Kollam in 849 | 849 (the plates' own witness list) | A Jewish merchant presence firm by 849; a chartered Jewish guild-lord at Kodungallur by c. 1000. The plates are real; their traditional date is off by ~620 years |
| Muslims | 629 CE (mosque founded in the Prophet's lifetime) | 849 CE — eleven Muslim witnesses in Kufic on the Quilon plates; mosque fabric/inscriptions from the 10th–12th c. | 782/783 burial (probable, epigraphic); 851 Arab shipping (Sulaiman); c. 915 settled community on the coast (Masudi, at Chaul); 1340s mosques in every port (Battuta) | A Muslim community firm by 849; mosques epigraphically visible 10th–12th c.; the Kodungallur building's oldest element 11th–12th c. The 629 foundation is unattested by any document within 880 years of its date |
| The ports themselves (Chinese notices) | — | — | Jia Dan's Canton–Gulf itinerary (c. 785–805) stages through Kollam; Zhou Qufei (1178) and Zhao Rugua (1225) describe Kollam (Gulin) as the pepper emporium where Arab and Chinese trade interlock; Wang Dayuan (1349), Ma Huan (1433) | Chinese records attest the trade system continuously from the 8th century and never name a religious foundation — the same silence as the Arabic geographers, from the other end of the route |

The pattern across the web is uniform. Every community's tradition date overshoots its attestation date, and the overshoot scales with the prestige of the claim: an apostle (52 → firm 849, overshoot ~800), the Prophet's own lifetime (629 → firm 849, overshoot ~220), a Solomonic-era aura for the plates (379 → c. 1000, overshoot ~620). And the documents, read without the traditions, tell one convergent story instead: in the 9th century the Abbasid-era trade system and the Chera Perumal charter state switch on together, and Christians, Jews and Muslims become simultaneously visible at Kollam — not because they arrived then, but because that is when this coast starts issuing documents that foreigners sign. Attestation dates are floors, not birthdays; but a floor is what a date claim must stand on, and 629 has none.

## 8. Verdicts

| Claim | Grade | Basis in one line |
|---|---|---|
| The Cheraman Juma Masjid was founded in 629 CE | **REJECTED as dated** | No document, inscription or fabric element within four centuries of 629; the legend's own earliest texts date the underlying event to c. 820s–900; the named founder is an anachronism (d. 748, of the generation after the Companions) |

| Muslim presence on the Malabar coast attested from 849 CE | **FIRM** | Eleven named Muslim witnesses signing in Kufic Arabic on the Quilon copper plates — a settled community of charter-witnessing standing at Kollam |
| Muslim presence on the coast by 782/783 CE | **PROBABLE** | The Panthalayani Kollam tombstone, AH 166 (misprinted "752 CE" in part of the literature) |
| Arab trade contact with the coast in the Umayyad century | **PROBABLE** | Umayyad coins at Kothamangalam; the pre-Islamic shipping pattern continuing — contact, not settlement |
| The Perumal conversion — a Chera king embraced Islam and left for Arabia | **UNRESOLVABLE as told; PLAUSIBLE only in a 9th–12th-c. form** | As told (Prophet's lifetime, moon, Malik ibn Dinar): charter legend — its own earliest sources refuse the date. As a bare event (a Perumal-era king converting under Arab merchant influence, c. 820s–1100s): possible, unattested, and unidentifiable with any named king |
| "First mosque in India" | **UNPROVABLE, and probably the wrong question** | Earlier-or-equal claims exist on both coasts (Kilakarai; the Gujarat coast, where Masudi's mosque-building community at Chaul is attested c. 915 and a Barwada/Ghogha foundation is traditionally dated to the 620s); no Indian mosque of any tradition carries a 7th-century dated element. Kodungallur's mosque is among India's oldest mosque sites by any honest reckoning — that is the strongest form of the claim the evidence will hold |

The correction here is not "Islam came late to Malabar." By the standards of the 9th-century world, a Muslim community witnessing a Hindu king's charter to a Christian church, in Arabic, in 849, is astonishingly early — earlier than almost anywhere else Islam reached by trade rather than conquest. What the record will not carry is the extra 220 years and the Prophet's lifetime. The coast's real distinction needs no loan from 629: it is the one place in India where three West-Asian religions become visible in the same document, on the same day, in 849 — and the mosque at Kodungallur is part of that world, dated by it, whether or not it was founded in it.

## 9. What would change these grades

- A dated inscription — Hijri, Kollam-era, or regnal — from the Kodungallur mosque or its site earlier than the 11th century would move the mosque's floor directly; a 7th–9th-century one would reopen everything.
- Scientific dating of the oldest timbers or the foundation (radiocarbon / OSL) has never been published for this building; a medieval-or-earlier result would convert the stylistic dating into a measured one.
- A pre-1500 textual witness to the conversion legend — an Arabic, Persian, Malayalam or Tamil manuscript demonstrably older than Barbosa, or a dated colophon for the *Qiṣṣat* — would compress the legend's stratigraphy and force re-grading of §2.
- Any 7th–9th-century Arabic source naming a Malabar convert king or a Kodungallur mosque would break the silence documented in §4.1, which is currently the strongest single argument against the early date.
- Conversely, a critical edition of the Panthalayani tombstone could move 782/783 either to firm or off the ladder entirely; the grade is explicitly hostage to that reading.
